## 🌿 The ecology hook

You never see a genotype directly in the field. You see phenotypes: size, colour, behaviour, survival. To learn what's underneath, you design assays: common gardens, reciprocal transplants, controlled crosses. You don't take the organism's appearance at face value.

A language model is the same kind of object. You only ever see its behaviour: the text it writes, the answer it picks, and sometimes a number it attaches to that answer (“I'm 90% sure”). Today is about designing an assay for that number. The question is simple: **when the model says 90%, is it right about 90% of the time?** If it is, the model is **calibrated**, and you can act on its confidence. If it isn't, that number is just more phenotype.

## 📖 Concept: is the confidence real?

### Calibration, in one sentence

> **Background (standard definitions, not from the pack):** a model is calibrated if, among all the answers it gives with confidence *p*, the fraction that are correct is close to *p*. A **reliability diagram** checks this: group answers into bins by stated confidence (say 0.5–0.6, …, 0.9–1.0), then plot each bin's actual accuracy against its average confidence. A calibrated model sits on the diagonal. Points below the diagonal mean **overconfidence**. The **expected calibration error (ECE)** is the average gap between the points and the diagonal, weighted by how many answers fall in each bin.

Accuracy and calibration are different properties. A model can be accurate but overconfident, or mediocre but honest about it. For automation, the honest one is often more useful, because you know when to trust it.

### What a calibrated decision model looks like

The clearest worked example in the pack is **Kev**, a family of small open-source “decision models” that answer yes/no, multiple-choice and rating questions with probabilities instead of prose [S:2101715352258232539]. Its README makes several points worth taking into every later module:

- **Each checkpoint ships with a fitted temperature.** The temperature is fitted on held-out data so that the probabilities match how often the model is right. You met this in m02: dividing every score by one number never changes which answer wins, only how confident the model sounds [S:2101715352258232539].
- **It measurably helps.** On data it never trained on, calibration cut one model's calibration error from 0.103 to 0.041, and its “confident errors” (wrong answers given with probability ≥ 0.9) from 8.2% to 2.4% [S:2101715352258232539].
- **It has a ceiling.** A single temperature can't reorder confidences. So it can't fix a model that ranks its right and wrong answers badly [S:2101715352258232539].
- **The Brier score** grades the whole probability distribution, not just the top answer, and lower is better [S:2101715352258232539]. (Background: for a yes/no question it's the mean squared difference between the stated probability and what happened, scored 1 or 0.)
- **Unknowable questions are a separate test.** Kev's benchmark includes records whose deciding evidence was removed, and reports how often a model still answers them with at least 0.9 confidence [S:2101715352258232539]. A model that confidently answers the unanswerable is the classic failure you want to catch.
- **A field called “confidence” is not automatically an accuracy rate.** The README spells out that its `confidence` fields are formulas over the probabilities, and that neither is a measured accuracy rate [S:2101715352258232539]. Read the label, then check what it measures.

The practical payoff is in how the probabilities are used: route the confident cases automatically and send the uncertain ones to a person. Kev's README states it plainly and adds a warning: test a probability threshold on your own data before you rely on it [S:2101715352258232539].

Unsloth's guide shows the same recipe from the training side. You fine-tune a small open model to score a fixed set of options, then calibrate it on held-out rows “so the model's probabilities match how often it's right” [S:2107868866361930236]. Their reported jump, from roughly chance-level accuracy (30–37%) to as high as 78% after one LoRA epoch, is a preview of Unit 9 [S:2107868866361930236]. Sebastian Raschka's long write-up on language models for text classification also treats calibration as a first-class result next to accuracy and efficiency [S:2104923910039040173]. (The pack only has his announcement post, not the write-up itself.)

### Chat models and stated confidence

When you *ask* a chat model how sure it is, you get a number it writes as text. Nothing guarantees that number was ever calibrated. TypeSafe, the company behind the Jev decision model, claims that chat models prompted for confidence “tend to be overconfident and inconsistent” [S:2105704739585565066]. That's a vendor arguing for its own product, so treat it as a hypothesis. Today's lab tests it on your own questions.

### Make errors easier to see

Calibration tells you *how often* to distrust an answer. The output format decides *how easily* you can find the error. Karpathy's tips for reading LLM output point the same way [S:2105819303471976479]:

1. **Controlled language.** Ask for explanations in ASD-STE100, a strict controlled English first written for aerospace maintenance manuals. If the full spec is too stiff, ask for something like “80% of the way” to it. Short, literal sentences are easier to check one claim at a time.
2. **Diagrams** instead of paragraphs.
3. **HTML pages**: interactive, visual explainers.
4. **Explainer videos**, which he's most bullish on.

His larger point: as models do more of the legwork, more of our work moves up into oversight and understanding. Cheap, throwaway artifacts (an HTML page built just to check one result) are now worth making [S:2105819303471976479]. Today's lab does exactly that: a reliability diagram built as a one-off web page.

```text
 stated confidence ─► bin ─► observed accuracy
  0.95, 0.92, 0.97   0.9–1.0     7 of 10 right = 0.70   ◄ below the diagonal: overconfident
  0.71, 0.66, 0.74   0.6–0.8     6 of 9 right  = 0.67   ◄ close to the diagonal: honest
```

> **Where the metaphor fails.** A phenotype was shaped by selection for survival, so it carries honest information about the organism, at least on average. A model's fluent explanation and its “I'm 90% sure” were shaped by training to be *helpful and convincing* to people. Fluency isn't evidence of correctness, and stated confidence isn't evidence of anything until you've assayed it. Also, an organism's phenotype doesn't change because you rephrased your question. A model's confidence can.

## 🧠 Self-check

**Q1. A model is right on 80% of questions. Is it calibrated?**
A: You can't tell from accuracy alone. It's calibrated only if its stated confidence matches its hit rate. For example, the answers it gives at 0.9 should be right about 90% of the time and the ones at 0.6 about 60%. An 80%-accurate model that says 0.99 on everything is overconfident.

**Q2. Kev fits a temperature on held-out data. What can that fix, and what can't it fix?**
A: It rescales confidence so that probabilities match observed accuracy, which lowers calibration error and the number of confident wrong answers. It can't change which answer wins or reorder which answers the model is more or less sure of, so a model that ranks its right and wrong answers badly stays bad at that.

**Q3. Why include unanswerable questions in a calibration test?**
A: They catch the most dangerous behaviour: confidently answering when the evidence isn't there. A well-calibrated system should give low confidence (or abstain) on them. Kev's benchmark reports how often a model still answers them at 0.9 or higher.

**Q4. You want an agent to auto-accept species IDs it is sure about and send the rest to you. What must you do before choosing the threshold?**
A: Measure it on your own labelled data: check accuracy at each confidence level (a reliability diagram) and pick the threshold that keeps the error rate among auto-accepted cases within your budget. Don't trust a vendor's number or a self-reported “confidence” field without testing.

**Q5. How does Karpathy's ASD-STE100 tip relate to calibration?**
A: Calibration tells you how often to distrust an answer. Controlled language (and diagrams or HTML) makes the answer easier to check claim by claim, so you find the errors faster when you do distrust it.
