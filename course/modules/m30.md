## 🌿 The ecology hook

Organisms don't only fit their niches; they build them. Beavers dam streams, earthworms remake soil, and the altered environment then shapes selection on the builders. **Niche construction** is a two-way fit, worked out over many generations.

Your capstone is a niche-construction project. You'll reshape one corner of your scientific work so that AI agents fit into it, and the work fits them. The final unit starts by choosing that corner carefully. Two sources guide the choice: a physicist who stopped treating Claude like a human collaborator and looked for "Claude-shaped" problems, and a forward-deployed engineer who always audits a process before automating it.

## 📖 Concept: Claude-shaped problems and the audit before automation

### The impedance mismatch

In a guest post on Anthropic's science blog, Harvard physicist Matthew Schwartz borrows a term from physics: an **impedance mismatch** is when two systems each work fine but are poorly matched, so most of what one puts in never gets through to the other. His claim is that current models are good at science but are not *scientists*, and that working with them as you'd work with a human collaborator isn't the best way to get their strengths [S:2105733864152858919]. What they do have, in his account: enormous breadth of knowledge, strong coding, mathematics and statistics, and the ability to parse papers and data at machine speed. What they can't yet do is help with deep conceptual questions [S:2105733864152858919].

So he went looking for "Claude-shaped" problems: places where a known technique from one field would solve a problem in another if anyone knew it existed, where lots of coding is needed, and, crucially, where the answer is **checkable**. His first project was chosen because anyone could verify the final answer to as many digits as they liked by running two scripts [S:2105733864152858919].

### An ecology case study, and the lesson in it

One of his projects was in your field. Etienne's 2005 equation turned Hubbell's neutral theory of biodiversity into something testable, but for twenty years nobody could solve it at scale. Claude recognised it as solvable with the toolkit and solved it. Applied to data from Barro Colorado Island, the result was that the tree-species mix changes 4.5 times faster than neutral theory allows [S:2105733864152858919].

Then came the expert. The plant biologist James O'Dwyer was impressed by the technical feat but said the result would likely be "met with a shrug by many ecologists", because ecologists already knew qualitatively that neutral theory can't keep up with real forests. His better idea was to subtract the neutral prediction and study the remainder, to see what is attributable to selection, competition and species differences. That led to a successor model that all three (two humans and Claude) were proud of [S:2105733864152858919]. Schwartz says almost every project followed this arc: technically correct, not very interesting, until an expert steered it [S:2105733864152858919].

His practical warnings will sound familiar by now [S:2105733864152858919]:

- **It loves to declare victory.** "Done, with one asterisk" often means not done. Set rigid standards for success.
- **Look at everything yourself.** Ask for plots; don't trust "good agreement".
- **Supply the taste.** It can find thousands of solvable problems but can't judge which are interesting.
- **Watch the grind.** It will grind through a multi-day calculation instead of building a tool that makes it take minutes, and it can't estimate time.
- His setup used separate sessions per project, a master session to coordinate, and a dedicated session "checking and rechecking results as an adversarial referee", with intermediate results stored in markdown files [S:2105733864152858919].

And the boundary: AI can sit inside the loop of acquiring, understanding and checking real-world data, but "it does not collapse the loop to a point" [S:2105733864152858919].

### Audit the real process before automating it

The forward-deployed-engineer (FDE) material in the pack comes from people who put agents into companies for a living. The rule is to audit first. One client used 17 steps to pay a single invoice; after a 2-3 week audit and redesign it was 7 steps, cycle time fell from 24 days to 6, and the share of invoices going straight through without an exception rose from 18% to 87%. "Agents were only one part of it" [S:2107228565519708665]. In Greg Isenberg's interview with the same practitioner, the real process is "almost always 3x longer than the one on paper" [S:2105793758616866834].

The method has three moves [S:2107228565519708665]:

1. **Map the real process.** Interview the people who do the work, with five questions per step: why do you do it this way? who decides? which step is theatre? what happens on an exception? how often? Then check the records, because what's on paper is not what happens: in one case a quote process documented as 7 steps turned out to have 20 steps and 7 loops.
2. **Sort every step into four buckets:** **delete** (shouldn't exist after AI), **plain code** (if X then Y, zero judgment), **agent** (needs judgment and you have historical examples), **human in the loop** (too risky to get wrong: approvals, payments, signing).
3. **Build inside the tools people already use**, and when a human must approve, send it where they already work.

Two numbers explain why step 1 matters. The audit cites Michael Hammer's study of an insurance application that took 22 days to move through a business and received 17 minutes of actual work: making every step twice as fast saves about eight minutes, because the days are spent in queues and handoffs [S:2107228565519708665]. And the practitioner's measurement rule: "We baseline before we build" [S:2105793758616866834], then use the cheapest model that does the job and come back to prove the result [S:2105793758616866834].

### What makes a good capstone niche

Putting both sources together, choose a workflow where:

- **the output can be verified** (a schema, a test, a source passage, your labelled items), because everything in Unit 8 depends on it;
- **there's history to learn from** (past datasheets, past triage decisions) for the agent steps;
- **the expensive judgment stays yours**: you supply the taste and approve the last step;
- **each unit's build has a job**: map (Unit 2), harness and router (3), verified loop (4), orchestrator and reviewer (5), skill and MCP (6), knowledge base (7), eval suite and adversary (8), decision model (9).

The suggested default is a **literature-triage and field-data-QA** team: triage new papers against your notebook, and check incoming field data against rules and past records.

> **Where the metaphor fails.** Niche construction is slow and mutual: organism and environment co-adapt over generations, and nothing is "deployed". Automating a workflow is fast and one-sided, and if the process is broken, automation just makes the broken process run faster. That's why the audit comes first and why "delete" is a bucket. Also, beavers don't need to measure their dam; you do. Without a baseline you'll never know whether your constructed niche is better than the old one.

**What to check in the result**

- Did the interview surface steps or loops you'd left off your Part A list? (The pack's practitioners found real processes much longer than the documented ones.)
- Is anything labelled **delete**? If not, look again for theatre.
- Does every **agent** step have a verification anchor? If one doesn't, it should probably be a human step or a plain-code rule.
- Is there exactly one human approval point, at the end, where the consequences are highest?
- Can you take the baseline measurements this week, before you build? Note them in `docs/baseline.md`; m32 measures again.

## 🧠 Self-check

**Q1. What does Schwartz mean by an "impedance mismatch" between scientists and AI?**
A: Both systems work well on their own but are poorly matched: treating the model like a human collaborator doesn't draw out its strengths (breadth, coding, maths and statistics, reading data fast), so much of the effort never gets through. The fix is to find problems shaped to what it does well.

**Q2. In the neutral-theory project, what did the ecologist add that the model didn't?**
A: Taste and direction. The model's result was technically impressive but would have been "met with a shrug", because ecologists already knew neutral theory falls short; the expert's idea to study the residual from the neutral prediction turned it into interesting science.

**Q3. Name the four buckets for sorting workflow steps, with one example each from your field.**
A: Delete (a step that's pure theatre, such as re-typing data already in a spreadsheet); plain code (a range check on a measurement); agent (judging whether an abstract is relevant to your project, given past decisions); human (approving the final dataset or a submission).

**Q4. Why can speeding up every step of a process achieve almost nothing?**
A: Because most elapsed time is often in queues, handoffs and exception loops, not the work itself (Hammer's 22 days containing 17 minutes of work). Removing steps and loops matters more than accelerating them.

**Q5. What makes a capstone problem "verifiable", and why does it matter?**
A: Its outputs can be checked against something independent of the model: a schema, a test, a source passage, labelled examples or a known value. Without that anchor, your evals, verifiers and rewards have nothing solid to measure against.
