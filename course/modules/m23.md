## 🌿 The ecology hook

When you want to know whether two genotypes really differ in fitness, you don't trust a field survey. You grow them side by side in a **common garden**: same soil, same water, same season, replicated plots, and a fitness measure fixed before the first seed goes in. Then any difference you see is about the genotypes, not the site.

An **eval** is the common garden for an AI system. You hold the tasks, the grader and the setup constant, change one thing (a prompt, a model, a skill file), and measure. Everything you've built so far in this course, from the agent loop to the RAG search, has been judged by eye. From today it gets an assay.

## 📖 Concept: what makes a good assay

### Three parts: tasks, grader, runner

An eval is a set of **tasks** (inputs that look like your real work), a **grader** (code or a model that scores each output) and a **runner** that executes every task and records a score plus the full transcript. The AI-engineer roadmap in this pack puts the starting point plainly: build a golden set of 20-50 representative inputs with expected outputs or rubrics, score them deterministically where you can (string match, JSON schema) and re-run the set every time you change a prompt or swap a model [S:2033587293064204349]. Its "critical mindset" line is worth keeping: every prompt change you make without running evals is a gamble [S:2033587293064204349].

### Four properties of a good eval

Anthropic's guide to eval design and hill-climbing lists four properties [S:2104676099083190435]:

1. **Tasks mirror production.** Sample what you actually care about, not what is easy to generate or easy to grade.
2. **Stronger models and more thinking score higher.** If they don't, suspect ambiguous tasks or a miscalibrated grader.
3. **There is "passable" headroom.** The best model at the highest effort should still be well below 100%, or you can't see improvements. But the gap must not come from impossible tasks: a task that fails on every run, however many replicates, is a tell. The guide's test for a good task is that two domain experts would reach the same verdict and everything the grader checks is stated in the task [S:2104676099083190435].
4. **Low run-to-run variance.** High variance usually means ambiguous tasks or a grader that gives different verdicts on identical output. It can also hide in the setup: leftover files or git history from an earlier trial can hand the agent the answer [S:2104676099083190435].

The guide adds a warning you'll recognise from sampling design. Model capability is **jagged**. If you pick test cases because today's model fails them, you sample the valleys of one model's capability surface, and the eval ends up measuring that model's "failure fingerprint" instead of what is hard or valuable for your work. Pick hard cases because a human judged them hard, and be able to say *why* each one is hard before you include it [S:2104676099083190435].

### Where the tasks come from

The guide's `build-eval` workflow samples inputs in this order: real production transcripts, then bug reports and tickets, then five to ten cases you write by hand, then cases synthesised from your codebase. Synthetic cases are fine if they're anchored in a few real examples, and you should see and approve every input before it's used [S:2104676099083190435]. For you, "production" means your real tasks: abstracts you triaged, field sheets you cleaned, questions you asked your notebook in m22.

### The cheapest grader that fits

If the output space is constrained (a label from a fixed set, JSON that matches a schema, tests that pass), use a **code grader**. Only when outputs are open-ended do you need an **LLM judge**, and then the judge should not be the model you're testing (that's tomorrow's module) [S:2104676099083190435]. Either way, read a sample of scored transcripts before you believe the grader: the guide calls scoring failures among the most common ways an eval is misconfigured [S:2104676099083190435].

### Noise: how big a change can you even see?

Every eval score is an estimate. Before you start improving anything, the guide checks that the eval's noise (how far the score can move by chance) is smaller than the smallest improvement you'd act on; if not, add repetitions or cases [S:2104676099083190435]. Results are reported with a **confidence interval**, and if a gain is within noise the advice is not to merge it [S:2104676099083190435].

> **Background (not from the pack; standard statistics):** a pass rate from *n* independent items behaves like a binomial proportion. With 10 held-out items, one item is 10 percentage points, and a 95% interval around 70% runs roughly from 40% to 89% (Wilson interval). So a small held-out set can only detect big effects. More items, or several runs per item, narrow the interval.

### Hill-climbing without fooling yourself

**Hill-climbing** means: change one thing, re-run the eval, keep the change if the score goes up. You met this search in m01; here the "landscape" is your prompt or configuration. The guide says it works best when the surface you're changing is **cheap to iterate** (text such as prompts and skills, not open-ended harness rewrites), the score change is **attributable** to that surface, and the objective is **well-scoped** (an eval near saturation is better used to cut cost at equal quality) [S:2104676099083190435].

The danger is **overfitting to the eval**. The guide describes how an eval can "leak" into the harness: hill-climbing adds features that fix the particular examples you chose but do nothing in production [S:2104676099083190435]. Three defences:

- **Split the cases.** A train set the hill-climber may read, a test set it never sees. Train up while test stays flat is the classic warning sign.
- **Never paste failures into the prompt.** Read failing transcripts to find causes, but don't copy their content in.
- **Keep the answers structurally out of reach**, because models can "reward hack" by finding the answers directly [S:2104676099083190435].

```text
          ┌───────────── one change (patch) ─────────────┐
          │                                              ▼
  baseline score ──► read TRAIN failures ──► run TRAIN + TEST
          ▲                                              │
          │  keep if both improve; revert if train↑ test flat, or any regression
          └──────────────────────────────────────────────┘
  stalled 2-3 rounds? stop editing, sort remaining failures by cause
```

That last step matters. When the score stalls, the guide's procedure stops editing and sorts every remaining failure by cause, which catches ambiguous tasks, harness errors and broken graders [S:2104676099083190435]. In Anthropic's own run on its claude-api skill, this step found a task whose grader demanded something the task never asked for, and a grader whose instructions contradicted the real API; fixing those (plus more skill edits) moved the score from 66% to about 88% [S:2104676099083190435].

The guide's worked example is a customer-support benchmark of 44 tickets: 30 for the search, 14 held out. The hill-climb removed contradictory rules from the prompt, then tried cheaper models and lower effort settings while checking that accuracy stayed above the baseline. On the 14 tickets the search never saw, the final setup scored 90.5% against the original's 78.6%, at about one fifth of the cost [S:2104676099083190435]. Note what that tells you: the honest number is the held-out one, and with only 14 items its interval is wide.

> **Tooling note.** Claude Code's built-in claude-api skill now has `/claude-api build-eval` and `/claude-api hillclimb` commands that run this whole procedure for you [S:2104676099083190435]. Today you'll build a small version by hand so you know what those commands are doing, and so the eval lives in your own capstone repo. Open-source frameworks such as Promptfoo (prompt and model comparison, CI checks, red teaming) do the same job at scale [S:2033587293064204349]. The awesome-evals list is a good annotated map of the wider literature [S:2105008692655743208].

> **Where the metaphor fails.** A common garden measures organisms that can't read the protocol. An AI system being tuned against an eval can, in effect, learn the protocol: every round of hill-climbing selects for whatever scores well on *these* items, including quirks of your grader. That's why a garden can be reused for decades but an eval needs a held-out split that the optimiser never sees, periodic rotation of items, and regular reading of transcripts. Also, your plots are independent replicates; eval items written by one person in one afternoon are not, so the real uncertainty is larger than the binomial interval suggests.

## 🛠 Lab: a 30-item assay and three rounds of hill-climbing (15 min)

You'll build an eval for a task you'll reuse three times: classifying a one-sentence study description by **design**. The labels are `experiment` (manipulative), `observational`, `model` (simulation/theory) and `synthesis` (review or meta-analysis). The same items come back in m24, m27 and the capstone.

**Part A (5 min, phone OK).** In your notes, write **eight** one-sentence study descriptions from your own field, two per label, each with its correct label. Make at least three of them genuinely tricky (for example, a "natural experiment" that's really observational, or a field survey that feeds a model). Next to each tricky one, write *why* it's hard. If you can't say why, it isn't a good hard case [S:2104676099083190435].

**Part B (10 min, at a computer).** This makes roughly 120 short headless calls on your subscription, so it uses some of your allowance. In Claude Code or Codex, paste (replacing the bracket with your eight items):

```text
Create ~/learn-ai/labs/m23 and work only inside it. Python standard library only.
1. items.jsonl: start with my 8 hand-written items below (fields: id, text, label, source="human").
   Then draft 22 more one-sentence ecology/evolution study descriptions anchored in the style of mine,
   balanced across the labels experiment / observational / model / synthesis (source="synthetic").
   Write review.html, a plain local page listing all 30 with their labels, and STOP so I can
   correct labels or delete bad items before anything runs.
2. After I confirm: split.py makes a seeded (seed 23) stratified split, 20 train / 10 test,
   saved as train.jsonl and test.jsonl. The hill-climber may read train only.
3. prompt.md: a short classification prompt that asks for exactly one label word.
4. run_eval.py: for each item, call `claude -p` (add `--model haiku` if that flag works,
   so there is headroom) or `codex exec` if claude is missing; 4 calls in parallel,
   120 s timeout, retry once on error. Grader = exact match on the lower-cased first word.
   Log one JSON line per item (input, raw output, parsed label, gold, pass) to runs/<round>.jsonl
   and count timeouts/errors separately from wrong answers.
   Print train and test accuracy, each with a 95% Wilson interval.
5. Hill-climb up to 3 rounds. Each round: read ONLY the train failures, propose ONE change
   to prompt.md as a diff, explain the root cause it targets, re-run train and test.
   Keep the change only if train and test both improve; revert if train improves but test is flat
   or if anything regresses. Never copy item text from failures into prompt.md.
6. Write results.md: a table of rounds (train %, test %, intervals, kept/reverted, cause),
   and a list of any items you now think are ambiguous or mislabelled.
My 8 items: [paste them here as "label | text"]
```

**What to check in the result**

- Open `review.html` before you confirm. Fix any label you disagree with: you are the domain expert, and two experts should agree on every item [S:2104676099083190435].
- Is the baseline already near 100%? Then there is no headroom: make some items harder (ask the agent to add harder variants of your tricky cases) before you climb.
- Look at the train-versus-test columns. Did any round improve train but not test? That's overfitting, and it should have been reverted.
- Compare the size of your improvement with the width of the test interval. With 10 test items, is the gain real or within noise?
- Read three failing transcripts yourself. Is the model wrong, or is the item ambiguous? Add any ambiguous items to results.md.

Keep the whole folder: m24 adds an LLM judge, m25 adds an adversary, and m27 uses `test.jsonl` to score a fine-tuned model.

## 🧠 Self-check

**Q1. Name the four properties of a well-designed eval from Anthropic's guide.**
A: Tasks mirror the real (production) distribution; stronger models and more thinking score higher; there is passable headroom (the best setup is well below 100%, but not because of impossible tasks); and run-to-run variance is low.

**Q2. Why is it a bad idea to build an eval from the cases today's model gets wrong?**
A: Capability is jagged, so you'd be sampling the valleys of one model's capability surface. The eval would measure that model's failure fingerprint rather than what is genuinely hard or valuable for your work. Pick hard cases because a human judged them hard, and be able to say why.

**Q3. During hill-climbing your train score rises from 70% to 85% but the held-out score stays at 70%. What do you do, and why?**
A: Revert the change. Train improving while test stays flat is the classic sign of overfitting: the change fitted quirks of the training items rather than improving the task in general.

**Q4. The score stalls for three rounds. What does the guide recommend instead of another edit?**
A: Stop editing and sort every remaining train failure by cause. This often reveals ambiguous tasks, broken graders or harness errors, which you fix in the eval itself, rather than spending rounds on changes too small to measure.

**Q5. You improved test accuracy from 7/10 to 8/10. Should you merge the change?**
A: Not on that evidence. One item out of ten is well within the noise of a 10-item set (the confidence intervals overlap heavily). Add items or repeated runs until the eval's noise is smaller than the improvement you care about.
