# m02 narration: Mutation rate, tokens and temperature

Welcome to Module Two.

Think about mutation rate. Too low, and a lineage is stable but stuck. Too high, and it explores wildly, but noise piles up and good solutions get lost. Somewhere in between is a useful balance, and the right balance depends on the job.

A language model faces that same trade-off at every single word it writes.

Yesterday was about training, how the weights are shaped. Today is about what happens when a trained model actually produces text.

First, a small piece of vocabulary. Models do not read words or letters. They read tokens, which are chunks of text, often pieces of words. Tokens are also how cost, usage limits and memory are counted, so you will hear that word a lot in this course.

Now the key idea. At each step, the model does not pick a word. It scores every possible next token, and turns those scores into a probability distribution. For a sentence like, perform the concerto for, blank, a good model spreads its bets over violin, piano and trumpet, and puts almost nothing on airplane. Then a separate piece of machinery, the sampler, draws one token from that distribution. The token is added to the text, and the whole thing repeats.

Temperature is the dial on that sampler. Low temperature sharpens the distribution, so the top choice almost always wins. High temperature flattens it, so rarer choices get through. In other words, temperature behaves a lot like a mutation rate.

In the widget you can move that dial and draw samples. Try a peaked distribution and a flat one, and count how many different words you get.

There is a twist worth remembering. When you need facts pulled out of a document, numbers from a field sheet, a yes or no decision, you want temperature at zero. Exploration there is a bug, not a feature. And low temperature does not make a model right. It only makes it consistent. It will repeat the same mistake every time.

That is where the metaphor fails, too. Mutations are inherited, and selection acts on them over generations. A temperature setting reshapes one draw at one step. Nothing is inherited, and nothing is selected, unless you build the selection yourself, for example by sampling several answers and keeping the one that passes a check. We will do exactly that in the unit on evals.

In today's lab, you will ask your coding agent to run the same question ten times, once vague and once precise, and measure how much the answers vary. You will see the prompt itself acting like a selective pressure.

Open the sampler, and start turning the dial.
