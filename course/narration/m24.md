# m24 narration: Judges, RAG metrics and tracing

Welcome to Module Twenty-Four.

If you have ever scored animal behaviour from video, you know this routine. Before anyone's scores count, two observers watch the same clips on their own. You compare their scores. You compute agreement. And whoever drifts gets retrained. Without that check, a rise in aggression might just mean a different person was watching.

Yesterday your grader was a few lines of code. It compared a label with the right answer, and it can never drift. But many outputs don't have one right form. A summary. An answer from your field notebook. A short synthesis of three papers. For those you need an observer, and more and more often, that observer is another language model. We call it a judge.

A judge is useful, and it is also a suspect. The research collected in this pack describes three classic biases. Judges tend to prefer whichever answer they read first. They tend to prefer longer answers. And they can recognise their own writing and favour it. So the rules are simple. Use code whenever you can. When you need a judge, give it a rubric made of checkable claims, ask for pass or fail rather than a score out of five, use a different model from the one you are testing, and check it against your own judgement before you trust it. Run it twice on the same answer, too. If the verdict changes, that is noise.

The second idea today is a diagnostic key for retrieval systems. Your field-notebook search can fail in two places. It can fetch the wrong evidence, or it can write an answer the evidence doesn't support. Two metrics look at the fetching: did the retrieved passages contain what the answer needs, and were the useful ones ranked first? Two look at the writing: is every claim supported, and does the answer address the question? Read them together, like a two-by-two table, and you know which half to fix.

The third idea is tracing. A run can finish without a single error and still be wrong. So you record everything: the question, the passages, the prompt, the answer, the verdicts, the time each step took. Then when something fails, you look at the path instead of guessing.

In the lab, you'll write expected answers for your ten questions from Module Twenty. Your coding agent will run your search, generate answers, score them with a judge from a different model family, and save a trace for every question. Then you will label ten answers yourself, and measure how well the judge agrees with you.

Start with Part A. Write your ten expected answers, in your own words, before you see anything the model says.
