# m01 narration: Loss is a fitness function

Welcome to Module One.

You already know how evolution works. Variation, selection and inheritance, repeated until a population climbs a fitness landscape. Today you will see that training a large language model is the same kind of search, turned upside down, and you will also see exactly where that comparison breaks.

Start with the landscape. In your field, height is fitness, and populations climb. In machine learning, height is loss. Loss is a single number that says how wrong the model is. So instead of climbing, the model descends.

What is it wrong about? Something very simple. Take real text, hide the next word, and ask the model to guess it. If the guess is good, little changes. If the guess is bad, that error becomes a signal, and the model is nudged so that next time it guesses better. Do that across a vast amount of text, and the guesses get remarkably good.

The model's genotype is its weights. Billions of numbers. The landscape is the loss over every possible setting of those numbers.

Here is the first big difference from evolution. A population discovers which way is uphill by trial and error. Mutants scatter, selection keeps the lucky ones. Training does something no organism can do. It calculates the slope directly. For every single weight, it works out which small change would lower the loss. That set of slopes is called the gradient, and following it downhill is called gradient descent.

In today's widget you will race two strategies across the same landscape. One follows the slope. The other is a simple evolutionary strategy that makes a handful of mutants each generation and keeps the best. Watch what happens when you make the landscape more rugged. Watch what happens when the step size is too big, or the mutations too small. You will find that each method fails in its own characteristic way.

Then comes the reading, and one habit I want you to build from day one. Some of the sources in this course are excellent, and some are hype wrapped around a good idea. One article today has a lovely explanation of optimization, and also some very precise numbers about a frontier model that nobody can trace. Keep the idea. Drop the numbers.

Finally, the lab. You will ask your coding agent, Claude Code or Codex, to write a small script that runs the race fifty times, from fifty random starting points, and reports how often each strategy finds the deepest valley.

By the end of today you should be able to explain, in plain words, why training a model is optimisation, not guessing, and why gradients beat mutation at this scale.

Open the widget, and let's start climbing down.
