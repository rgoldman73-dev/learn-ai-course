# m30 narration: Choosing your niche

Welcome to Module Thirty, and to the capstone.

Organisms don't only fit their niches. They build them. Beavers dam streams. Earthworms remake the soil. And the changed environment then shapes selection on the builders. You know this as niche construction, a two-way fit worked out over generations.

Your capstone is a niche-construction project. You'll reshape one corner of your scientific work so that AI agents fit into it, and so that the work fits them. Today is about choosing that corner well.

The first guide is a physicist, Matthew Schwartz, writing on Anthropic's science blog. He describes an impedance mismatch. Two systems that each work fine, but are badly matched, so most of what one puts in never reaches the other. His point is that these models are good at science but are not scientists. They have huge breadth, strong coding, maths and statistics, and they read papers and data at machine speed. What they can't do yet is the deep conceptual part. So he stopped treating Claude like a graduate student and went looking for problems shaped to its strengths, problems where the answer can be checked.

One of those problems was in your field. Neutral theory of biodiversity. An equation that had resisted solution at scale for twenty years was solved, and applied to the forest plot on Barro Colorado Island. Technically impressive. But when he showed it to an ecologist, the verdict was that most ecologists would shrug. They already knew neutral theory falls short. The ecologist's better idea was to study what's left after you subtract the neutral prediction, and that became real science. Schwartz says nearly every project went this way. The model supplied the horsepower. The expert supplied the taste.

The second guide comes from engineers who put agents into companies for a living. Their rule is to audit before you automate. One client needed seventeen steps to pay a single invoice. After the audit it was seven, and agents were only one part of the fix. Their method is to map the process as it really happens, which is usually far longer than the version on paper. Then sort every step into four buckets. Delete it. Turn it into a simple rule. Give it to an agent. Or keep a human on it. And measure before you build, so you can prove the change later.

Here's where the metaphor breaks. Niche construction is slow and mutual. Automation is fast, and if the process is broken, it simply runs the broken process faster.

In the lab, your coding agent will interview you about one workflow you really do, step by step. Then it sorts the steps, finds a checkable anchor for every agent step, plans your baseline measurements, and writes a one-page capstone spec that gives each of your earlier builds a job.

Start with Part A. Choose one recurring workflow, and list the steps as you actually do them, including the annoying ones.
