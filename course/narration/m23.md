# m23 narration: Designing a fitness assay

Welcome to Module Twenty-Three, and to Unit Eight: fitness assays.

Think about the last time you wanted to know whether two genotypes truly differed. You didn't trust a survey of wild plants growing in different places. You grew them side by side in a common garden. Same soil, same water, same season. Replicated plots. And a fitness measure you fixed before the first seed went in.

An eval is the common garden for an AI system. You hold the tasks, the grader and the setup constant. You change one thing, maybe a prompt, a model or a skill file. Then you measure. Until now, almost everything you have built in this course has been judged by eye. From today, it gets an assay.

Anthropic's guide to eval design gives four properties of a good one. The tasks should look like your real work, not like whatever was easy to write. Stronger models should score higher. There should be headroom, so the best system still misses some items. And the score should not jump around from run to run.

There is a sampling trap here that you will recognise. If you build your test set from the cases today's model gets wrong, you are sampling the valleys of one model's abilities. Your eval ends up describing that model's weak spots, not what is hard in your field. So choose hard cases because you, the expert, judge them hard, and be able to say why.

Then comes hill-climbing. Change one thing, re-run, keep it if the score improves. It is the same search you met on day one. But now the optimiser can overfit. It can tune itself to the quirks of your particular test items. The defence is a held-out split. One set of items the optimiser may study, and another it never sees. If the training score rises while the held-out score stays flat, you revert the change.

And remember noise. With ten held-out items, one item is ten percentage points. A small gain may be nothing but chance. Report a confidence interval, and only trust improvements bigger than your noise.

In the lab you will write eight study descriptions from your own field, labelled by design: experiment, observational, model or synthesis. Your coding agent will add more, you will check every label, and then it will run three careful rounds of hill-climbing on a prompt, with a train and test split and intervals on every score.
