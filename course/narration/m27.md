# m27 narration: Selective breeding

Welcome to Module Twenty-Seven.

A breeder never designs a new animal from scratch. They start from a stock that already has most of what they want, and they shift it, carefully, towards one trait. And much of what a young animal knows, it never got from selection at all. It learned by imitating its parents. Song dialects. Foraging tricks. Migration routes. Cultural transmission is fast and faithful, and it passes on the parents' mistakes right along with their skills.

Supervised fine-tuning is cultural transmission for a model. You show it examples of the behaviour you want, and you nudge its weights so it imitates them. A lecture in this pack describes it in one phrase: imitation learning. The model is graded on every word of the example answers, and over many examples it learns to produce answers like them.

The lecture also lists the catches. A student can't outgrow its teacher. Wrong examples are learned as faithfully as right ones. Even a successful example can carry bad habits. Adding more of the same examples stops helping after a while, while new kinds of examples keep helping. And a falling training loss is not the same as a better model. You still need your held-out eval.

Then there's a technique called LoRA, which is the breeder's restraint. Instead of changing the whole model, you freeze it, and you train a small add-on that sits beside it. The add-on is tiny, so it trains on a free graphics card and can be saved and swapped on its own.

Several projects in the pack use this to build what they call decision models. Instead of writing an answer in words, the model scores the options you give it, and returns a choice with a probability. One small model went from about chance to around three quarters correct on its tests after a single short round of training. Another project warns that where you start matters. Training on a narrow set of examples from the bare base model wiped out much of what a ready-made decision model already knew.

Here's where the metaphor breaks. Breeding selects among variation that already exists. Fine-tuning doesn't select. It copies. If your labels are wrong, the model learns them perfectly.

In the lab, you'll do this for your own study-design task. Your coding agent will generate a few hundred practice examples, filter them with a second blind labeller, and build a free notebook that trains a small model in the cloud. Then you'll compare the untrained and trained models on your human-checked items from Module Twenty-Three, and nowhere else.
