# m20 narration: Foraging for facts

Welcome to Module Twenty, and to the unit on the field notebook: retrieval and memory.

You know the forager's dilemma. In a patchy landscape, every patch starts rich and gets poorer the longer you stay. Charnov's marginal value theorem says leave when your rate of gain in this patch drops below what you could expect elsewhere, counting the travel. Rich patches deserve long visits. Poor ones, a quick look and move on.

An agent answering questions from documents is a forager. Its documents are cut into chunks, those are the patches. A search engine ranks them. The agent works down the list. And every chunk it reads costs context and attention. The first few hits are usually the richest. At some point, reading one more chunk is worth less than it costs.

Fetching the right evidence before answering has a name: retrieval augmented generation, or RAG. And today, you'll use the source pack behind this very course as your first RAG system.

The classic way to rank chunks is called B M twenty-five. It's keyword search, done cleverly, and it rests on three ideas. First, rare words matter more than common ones. A chunk that contains the word compaction is far more telling than one that contains the word agent. Second, repetition helps, but with diminishing returns. Five mentions beat one, but not by five times. Third, long chunks get a penalty, because they contain more words just by chance. Two dials control the last two ideas, and in today's widget you can turn them and watch the ranking change.

You might expect keyword search to be old-fashioned. But one of today's sources found that none of the eleven coding agents it studied use embedding search to find code. They use plain text search, and it works, because code and technical writing are full of exact names.

The other big decision is chunk size. Too large, and each chunk covers several topics, so the right one is hard to pick out. Too small, and the idea gets cut in half. Overlapping chunks a little helps with the cut.

Here's where the foraging metaphor fails. A forager learns. It remembers which patches paid off and changes its behaviour. B M twenty-five learns nothing at all. The same question gets the same ranking forever. And it doesn't know what words mean. Ask about an agent forgetting its earlier rules, and it may never find the passage about compaction, because the words don't match.

In the lab, you'll write ten questions about things you've learned in this course, run them through the pack's search tool, and judge the top five results yourself. That gives you a number, precision at five, and a little test set that you'll reuse for the rest of the unit. Then your coding agent will try different dial settings to see whether anything beats the default.
