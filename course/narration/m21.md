# m21 narration: Smelling the patch

Welcome to Module Twenty-One.

Yesterday's forager could only find food by an exact landmark. Many animals do better than that. A shearwater flying over open ocean can't see the krill beneath the surface. But it can smell the plume of dimethyl sulphide that rises where plankton are being grazed, and it flies up the gradient. It doesn't need an exact match. It needs a signal that grows stronger as it gets closer to what it wants.

Embeddings give search that same kind of sense.

Here's the idea. A small model turns every chunk of text into a point in a space with hundreds of dimensions. It places those points so that passages with similar meanings end up close together. Your question becomes a point too. Searching then means finding the nearest chunks, even if they don't share a single word with your question. Ask about an agent forgetting its earlier rules, and the passage about compaction can finally turn up.

These models can be small. One announced this month runs on a laptop in about half a gigabyte of memory. One practical warning from today's sources: choose your embedding model before you build the index, and stick with it. Points made by one model mean nothing to another. Switch models, and you have to re-embed everything.

So why not use embeddings alone? Because each sense has blind spots. Keyword search can't see synonyms. Embeddings can blur exact names, numbers and identifiers. And they find passages that are about the same topic, which is not the same as passages that answer your question.

The answer most production systems use is hybrid search. Run both searches, and combine the two ranked lists with a simple method called reciprocal rank fusion. It ignores the raw scores, which aren't comparable, and looks only at where each passage ranked in each list. Something near the top of both lists rises to the top.

Here's where the smell metaphor breaks. A scent gradient is anchored in real chemistry. The plume really is stronger near the food. Embedding similarity is learned from patterns in text. Near means written about in similar ways, not true, and not useful. A fluent passage full of the right words can sit closer to your question than the dry paragraph that actually has the answer. The only way to know is to check.

So that's the lab. You'll install a small, free embedding model, embed the course's source pack, and build a hybrid search. Then you'll run yesterday's ten labelled questions through three searches, keywords, embeddings and the hybrid, and compare their precision at five. Look question by question, not just at the average. That's where you'll see each sense earning its keep.

Let's go and follow the scent.
