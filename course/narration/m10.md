# m10 narration: Reflex and cortex

Welcome to Module Ten, the last module of Unit Three.

Touch a hot stove, and your hand is already moving before you feel the pain. A reflex arc in your spinal cord made that decision in a few thousandths of a second. Your brain hears about it afterwards. Nervous systems are layered like that. Cheap, fast circuits for routine stimuli. Expensive deliberation for everything else.

Right now, your agent has no reflexes. Every decision, big or small, goes to the same model at the same price. Today you give it a reflex layer, called a router. Its job is to look at a task and decide which model it actually needs.

One team at LangChain did exactly this for their internal coding agent, because their bill kept climbing. First they studied a week of real requests and found that difficulty varied enormously. Then they picked three models, a fast one, a balanced one, and a strong one. Then they wrote a short router. A brief instruction, pick the least expensive model likely to finish the task, plus one line describing each tier, and a small classifier to make the call.

Then they tested it properly. Half the work went through the router, and half always went to the strongest model. The share of work that ended in an accepted change was essentially the same. But the typical cost per task fell by almost two thirds. And when they tried the opposite, sending everything to the cheapest model, engineers complained within a day. Routing is not about using the cheap model. It is about using the right one.

Two details matter. The router decides once, at the start of a task, because switching models halfway through throws away the cache you learned about in Unit Two. And the router has to be evaluated, like any other model.

That last point is where some of today's sources are refreshingly honest. One builder ran a skill router quietly for a week. Out of more than five hundred suggestions, only about one in twenty was used. He removed it. Another found that his routed helpers were cheaper than one setting, but more expensive than simply running a single strong agent. A router is a hypothesis. You have to test it against the right comparison.

And here is where the metaphor fails. Reflexes were tuned by selection over millions of years, and when a reflex misfires, you usually notice. A router is only as good as the examples and tests behind it. And when it sends a hard task to a weak model, nothing flinches. You just get a plausible, slightly worse answer.

In today's lab you will write twenty tasks and label how hard each one is. You will build two routers, one from simple rules and one using the cheapest model, and score both. Watch especially for under-routing, where a hard task is sent to a weak model. Then you will wire the router into your agent. That completes your Unit Three harness.

Open your terminal, and let's build a reflex.
