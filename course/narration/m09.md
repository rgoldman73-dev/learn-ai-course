# m09 narration: Behavioural repertoires

Welcome to Module Nine.

You know the classic example of a fixed action pattern. A goose sees an egg outside the nest and rolls it back with its bill. The sequence runs the same way every time, even if you take the egg away halfway through. It is cheap, fast, and reliable in the world it evolved for. Flexible foraging is the opposite. The animal samples, adjusts, and decides patch by patch. It copes with surprises, but it costs more.

Most animals use both. And when you build with language models, you should too.

Yesterday you built an agent that decides its own next step. Today's question is when to let it decide at all.

Anthropic draws a clear line between two kinds of system. In a workflow, you write the sequence of steps in code, and the model fills in each step. In an agent, the model decides the sequence itself. And their main advice is almost blunt. Find the simplest solution that works. Add complexity only when it clearly improves the results. Sometimes that means no agent at all.

They describe five workflow patterns. A chain, where one step feeds the next, with checks in between. A router, which sorts each input to a specialised path. Parallel work, either splitting a job into independent pieces, or running the same job several times and comparing. An orchestrator, which breaks a job down on the fly and hands out the pieces. And an evaluator, where one model drafts and another critiques, round after round.

Agents are for open-ended problems, where you cannot predict the steps. They are powerful, but they cost more, they vary from run to run, and their mistakes can build on each other.

There is also evidence that fixed pipelines can hold their own. One well-known pipeline with three fixed steps, find the problem, repair it, check it, was reported to be competitive with free-roaming agents on a standard coding benchmark.

So where does the metaphor fail? The goose did not choose its egg-rolling routine. Selection fixed it over countless generations, and running it costs nothing extra. You choose, today, for each task, and flexibility is paid for on every single run. And unlike the goose, you can open up a workflow and test each step. That inspectability is often the real reason to prefer one.

In today's lab you will run a literature-triage task two ways. You will make twenty short abstracts with an answer key you check yourself. Then you will triage them with a fixed chain, extract, check, decide, and with a free agent that plans its own approach. You will compare accuracy, time and number of calls, and then read every mistake. Notice how much easier it is to find where the chain went wrong.

Open your terminal, and let's compare repertoires.
