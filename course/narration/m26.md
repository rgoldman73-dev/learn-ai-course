# m26 narration: Domestication at home

Welcome to Module Twenty-Six, and to Unit Nine: artificial selection.

Domestic strains are rarely the most impressive members of their lineage. A bantam is smaller than a junglefowl. A lab strain of Arabidopsis would lose in the wild. What you gain is a strain that fits your conditions. Your bench, your greenhouse, your budget, under your control.

Running a language model on your own computer is the same bargain. You give up the biggest, smartest models. In return, you get a model that lives on your hardware. Your data never leaves the machine. Nobody can change your limits. An extra request costs nothing. And the model stays exactly the same until you decide to replace it. One course in this pack is honest about the trade. You don't go local to save money per word. You go local for control, and for volume you don't have to meter.

Two hardware facts explain almost everything. To write each word, the program reads every active weight of the model out of memory once. So the size of your memory decides which models will load at all. And the speed of that memory sets the ceiling on how fast the words come out.

Then there's a trick for fitting a bigger model into less memory. It's called quantisation. Weights normally ship as sixteen-bit numbers. Store them with four bits instead, and a model shrinks to about a third of its size. Often you can't measure the loss. But not always. One heavily compressed model in the pack claims ninety-eight percent of the original's average score, and yet on a long, multi-step agent test it falls far behind. Averages can hide the one drop you care about. So you test on your own tasks.

There's one more memory cost people forget: the context. Every word the model is holding in its working memory takes space. Raise the context window, and memory climbs, even if your prompt is short.

In the lab, you'll install a free local runtime and a small model of about four billion parameters. Then you'll run a careful experiment, the way you would in a growth chamber. Change one setting at a time. Short prompt versus long prompt. Small context versus large. Three replicates each. Record reading speed, writing speed and memory. And finally, run the ten held-out items from your Module Twenty-Three eval through the local model, and compare its accuracy with what you measured before.

By the end, you'll know whether this little strain is good enough for the simple, repetitive decisions in your future capstone.
