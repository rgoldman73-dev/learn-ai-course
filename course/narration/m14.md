# m14 narration: Manager versus handoffs

Welcome to Module Fourteen, and to the unit on food webs: systems with more than one agent.

Ask most people how an ant colony works, and they'll tell you about a queen giving orders. You know that's wrong. The queen mostly lays eggs. The real coordination happens from the bottom up. A forager lays pheromone on a trail, others follow the strongest trail, and the trail itself becomes the message. Coordination through traces in a shared environment. Stigmergy.

Both pictures, the boss and the trail, show up when people connect AI agents together.

In the first, the manager pattern, one agent stays in charge. It calls specialist agents the way it would call a tool, collects their answers, and speaks to you with one voice.

In the second, the decentralised pattern, agents hand off. A triage agent sees that your question is about an order, passes the whole conversation to the orders specialist, and steps out of the way.

And many real systems add a shared whiteboard that every agent reads and writes. That is stigmergy by another name.

Now the most important lesson of the day, which comes straight from OpenAI's guide. Start with one agent. Give it good tools and clear instructions, and only split it when it really struggles. Usually that's when its instructions have grown into a tangle of special cases, or when it keeps choosing the wrong tool because several tools look alike.

When you do split, you gain things. Each sub-agent needs less context. Simple subtasks can go to cheaper models. Independent work can run in parallel. But you also lose things. Information gets dropped at every handoff, and a fixed plan can't adapt. In one experiment from the CMU course, an agent that had to stick to its first plan succeeded about a quarter of the time, and letting it revise the plan helped a lot.

A good rule of thumb: the signal for a team is width. Thirty papers to compare, not one.

Here's where the colony comparison fails. A colony's coordination was shaped by millions of years of selection, and it survives losing workers. Your topology was designed last Tuesday. Every extra agent is another bill and another place to break. And pheromone evaporates on its own, so stale trails fade. A shared whiteboard does not fade unless you make it.

In the lab, you will put three papers you know well into a folder and build a small manager. It sends each paper to its own summariser, running in parallel, checks their answers with ordinary code, and then asks for one synthesis. Then you'll run a single agent on the same papers and compare. Because you know these papers, you get to be the judge.

Pick your three papers, and let's build a colony.
