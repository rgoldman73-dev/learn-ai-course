# m13 narration: Approve only the last step

Welcome to Module Thirteen.

Picture a well-run field station. The data loggers record temperature every ten minutes, whether anyone is awake or not. The technicians walk the weekly transects from a written protocol. Only two people hold the key to the deep freezer. A script flags impossible readings before anyone looks at them. And the principal investigator does not check every quadrat. They sign off one thing: the final dataset, before it goes out into the world.

That is exactly the shape you want for your agents. Routine work runs on its own, inside written rules. Mechanical checks catch the errors. And your signature goes on the last, irreversible step, and nowhere else.

Today you learn the four tools that make that possible.

The first is instructions. You already know the CLAUDE dot M D file. It's useful, but it is a suggestion. Under pressure, a model can forget it.

The second is permissions. You decide what the agent may do freely, what needs your approval, and what is simply blocked. Running tests? Fine. Pushing to the shared repository, deleting raw data, reading your password file? Blocked.

The third is hooks. A hook is a command the harness runs automatically, at a fixed moment, every single time. Run the tests after every edit. Refuse a dangerous command before it happens. One of today's sources puts it neatly. An instruction is a suggestion. A hook always runs.

The fourth is headless mode. You hand the agent one task, it works with nobody watching, and it stops. That is where your rules stop being a convenience and become the whole safety story.

Then comes the most important design question. Where does the human go? The answer from today's reading is simple. Put yourself at the point of highest consequence and lowest reversibility. Approve the merge. Not every step along the way. And decide what can flow automatically by asking how hard a mistake would be to undo, not by asking how confident the model feels. The model can talk itself into confidence. It cannot talk its way out of an irreversible deletion.

Here is where the field station comparison breaks. Your technician understands why the freezer key is restricted, and fills gaps in the protocol with good judgement. An agent follows the letter of its permissions. Block one delete command, and it may delete the file another way, not out of malice, but because that path was open. So the hard limits belong in places it cannot route around.

In the lab, you will build a small field data cleaning project with tests, permission rules, and a hook that runs the tests after every edit. You will break a test on purpose, try a forbidden command, and then run one task with nobody watching. Your only job at the end is to read the changes and make the commit yourself.

That repository becomes part of your capstone. Open the reading, and then go build your field station.
