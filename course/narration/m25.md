# m25 narration: The Red Queen

Welcome to Module Twenty-Five.

"It takes all the running you can do, to keep in the same place." You know why evolutionary biologists borrowed that line from the Red Queen. Hosts and parasites, predators and prey, keep each other honest. Take the predators away, and the prey slowly lose their vigilance. A defence that is never tested decays.

AI systems need opponents too. And today's rule is the most important one in this unit. Never let the generator grade its own homework.

Here is why. Research cited in one of today's sources shows that language models can recognise their own writing, and that they tend to prefer it. So when a model checks its own work, it is a lenient referee by design. One builder in the pack described exactly this. Every worker in their system checked its own output, and everything looked clean. Then they split the job. A separate checker, with one task: try to kill each finding before it moves on.

Three details made that checker work. First, a fresh context. When the checker was handed the worker's whole conversation, it agreed with everything, because it had already been talked into the answer. Second, several lenses and a vote. One checker looks at correctness, one at the sources, one at overclaiming, and the majority decides. Third, an anchor outside the models. If every checker compares a number against the same summary, their agreement is just an echo. The anchor is the original source, the raw data, or a test that actually runs.

And then, test the tester. Break something on purpose and make sure your checker notices. If a deliberately broken answer passes, your checker is blind, and every green tick after that means nothing.

In the lab, you'll do exactly that. Your coding agent will write a short report of twelve claims, each citing a passage from this pack. A script will then plant six errors: a changed number, a reversed claim, a wrong citation, and others, including one typical of your own field. Then three checkers try to find them. The writer checking itself. An independent verifier that sees only the claim and its source. And an adversary that tries three different ways to disprove each claim.

You'll count what each one catches, and what each one wrongly flags, and you'll put a confidence interval on every number. With only six planted errors, those intervals will be wide. That's part of the lesson. Robotics labs now publish success rates from thousands of trials, with intervals, for exactly this reason.

Start with Part A. Think of one mistake you often see in papers in your field, and write it down as your sixth error type.
