# m28 narration: Selection on behaviour

Welcome to Module Twenty-Eight.

Yesterday was imitation. Copy the parent. Today is selection on behaviour. An individual tries something, the environment scores the outcome, and behaviours that pay off become more common. No teacher needed, which means behaviour can go beyond anything a teacher ever showed.

That's reinforcement learning. The model is called the policy. It plays out a whole episode, which we call a rollout: act, observe, act again. At the end, a reward, one number, says how well it went. Then the policy is nudged so that actions from better-than-expected rollouts become more likely.

Better than expected is the key phrase. A lecture in this pack shows why. Raw rewards are noisy, so you compare each rollout with a baseline, a sense of what the policy usually gets. One popular method gets that baseline very simply. It plays the same task about eight times and uses the average reward of the group. Rollouts above the average are reinforced. Rollouts below it are discouraged.

There's a catch you'll recognise from selection in small populations. If every rollout in the group fails, there's nothing to select between. No variation, no response. Reinforcement learning can only amplify what the model can already sometimes do. So people start with easier tasks, or give hints, or teach by imitation first.

And now the dark side. Selection acts on whatever is measured. Select hens for eggs per cage, and you may select the hen that bullies her neighbours. In machine learning this is called reward hacking, and it's Goodhart's law with a training loop attached. The pack includes an audit of coding agents that looked successful but had often just looked up the known fix. When the shortcuts were blocked, their scores dropped sharply. One research team puts the lesson plainly: verification has to keep evolving alongside the model it checks. That's the Red Queen again, now inside training.

In the lab, your coding agent will build the guessing game from the lecture. A hidden number from one to sixteen, four guesses, higher or lower. A tiny policy will learn to play it. You'll compare learning with and without a baseline. Then you'll plant a bug in the reward checker, so that one illegal guess also counts as correct, and you'll watch the reward climb while the real success rate falls.

Finally, you'll package the small decision model from the last two modules into one function your capstone can call, with a gate that only lets it act if it passes your held-out test.
