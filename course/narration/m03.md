# m03 narration: Reading the organism

Welcome to Module Three.

In the field, you never see a genotype directly. You see phenotypes. Size, colour, behaviour, who survives the winter. And you have learned not to take appearances at face value. When you want to know what is really underneath, you design an assay. A common garden. A reciprocal transplant. A controlled cross.

Today, the organism is a language model, and the phenotype is its confidence.

Ask a model a question and it may say, I am ninety percent sure. That sounds like information. But is it? Here is the test. Collect every answer the model gave at ninety percent. Are about nine in ten of them actually right? If they are, the model is calibrated, and you can act on that number. If only six in ten are right, the model is overconfident, and the number is decoration.

Notice that this is a different question from accuracy. A model can be right most of the time and still be badly calibrated. And for automation, calibration is often what matters most. If you know when a model is unsure, you can send those cases to a person and let the confident ones through.

One of today's sources is a small open-source decision model called Kev. Its builders fit a single calibration setting on data the model never trained on. It does not change which answer wins. It only changes how sure the model sounds. On new data, that one adjustment cut its confident mistakes from about eight in a hundred to between two and three. They also test the model on questions that cannot be answered at all, to see whether it still claims to be sure. That is a trick worth stealing.

The second idea today comes from Andrej Karpathy, and it is about reading outputs, not scoring them. Ask for explanations in a strict, simple controlled English, the kind written for aircraft maintenance manuals. Or ask for a diagram. Or a small web page. Short, literal statements are easier to check one at a time.

And here is where the metaphor fails. A phenotype was shaped by selection, so on average it tells you something honest about the organism. A model's fluent, confident paragraph was shaped by training to be helpful and convincing. Fluency is not evidence. Confidence is not evidence either, until you have tested it.

In the lab, you will have your coding agent write forty yes or no questions from your own field, plus five that cannot be answered. You will check the answer key yourself. Then a fresh model answers them with a confidence for each, and you get a reliability diagram as a little web page. Finally, you will gather this whole first unit into a notebook you can come back to.

Open the lab, and start your assay.
