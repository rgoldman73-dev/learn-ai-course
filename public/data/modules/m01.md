## 🌿 The ecology hook

You already think in fitness landscapes. You imagine a population sitting on a surface of genotypes, with height as fitness. Mutation scatters offspring around the parent, selection keeps the better ones, and over generations the population climbs, unless it gets stuck on a local peak.

Training a language model is the same kind of search, turned upside down: instead of climbing **fitness**, it descends **loss**, a number that measures how wrong the model's predictions are. The genotype is the model's **weights** (billions of numbers). The landscape is the loss surface over all possible weight settings. And the search method is usually not mutation and selection but **gradient descent**.

## 📖 Concept: what “training” actually is

### The objective: guess the hidden next word

A modern language model is trained on a very simple game. Take real text, hide the next word, and ask the model to predict it. If it guesses well, little changes; if it guesses badly, that error becomes a signal that adjusts the model so it guesses better next time. Jeff Dean's lecture describes exactly this self-supervised setup, and notes why it scales: the world contains so much text that you get almost unlimited training examples where the right answer is known [S:2106489085829488811]. Some next words are hard (“Princeton ___” could continue in many ways) and some are easy, so the loss is high on some positions and low on others [S:2106489085829488811].

The CMU agents course states the same thing from the agent side: at each step the model predicts a **distribution over the next token**, and something samples or otherwise chooses one [S:2104635196910030894]. (Tokens, distributions and sampling are tomorrow's module.)

So the **loss function** is the fitness function, inverted: one number that says how badly the current weights predict the training text.

### The search: follow the slope

Backpropagation computes, for every weight, which direction would make the loss go down, and by how much. That vector of slopes is the **gradient**. Gradient descent then takes a small step downhill, recomputes, and repeats, for many steps over many batches of text [S:2106489085829488811].

The “LLMs do not guess, they optimize” article gives a good lineage for this. Newton's method uses both the slope and the curvature (the second derivative) to jump towards a minimum and converges very fast. Gradient descent is a stripped-down Newton: it ignores curvature and just follows the slope. You lose fast convergence, but you gain the ability to handle functions with trillions of variables without ever building a matrix the size of the parameter space [S:2106716291708911844]. The same article points out that when you *can't* get a derivative (for example when choosing a learning rate, where each trial means training a model), you fall back on derivative-free searches that only evaluate the function at chosen points [S:2106716291708911844].

That is the key contrast for today:

| | Gradient descent | Evolutionary strategy |
|---|---|---|
| Information used | exact local slope at one point | only fitness values of sampled points |
| Population | one lineage | parent + offspring each generation |
| Cost per step | one gradient (in real networks about as cheap as a couple of predictions) | λ fitness evaluations |
| Typical failure | stuck in the nearest basin; overshoots if the step is too big | slow; wastes evaluations; still can stall on rugged ground |
| Biological echo | none, really: no organism can read off the gradient | mutation + selection |

> **Background (not from the pack):** evolution strategies are a real family of optimisers, used when gradients are unavailable or unreliable. For training large neural networks, gradient descent wins because backpropagation gives the full gradient for roughly the cost of a few forward passes, no matter how many weights there are.

### Flat basins and the hype filter

Where training ends up matters. The loss-landscape post argues that sharp, narrow minima tend to memorise and generalise poorly, while wide, flat basins generalise better, and that the shape of the landscape changes with scale [S:2106716291708911844]. Sharp versus flat minima is a real and widely discussed idea. But the same post also quotes very precise figures for a frontier model's training cost, steps and “generalization gap” with no traceable source. Treat those numbers as unverified: keep the concept, drop the figures. This is a habit you'll use all course long.

> **Where the metaphor fails.** A fitness landscape is walked by a *population*, over *generations*, by organisms that never see the slope. Training walks a single lineage of weights, and it gets an exact local direction at every step, something mutation never provides. The landscape has billions of dimensions, where true local peaks are rarer than in your 2-D sketch and long flat ridges and saddle points dominate. And the “fitness” is chosen by people: next-word loss is a proxy for what we actually want (helpful, correct answers), which is why later modules spend so much time on evals and Goodhart's law.

## 🧠 Self-check

**Q1. In next-word training, what plays the role of the fitness function, and what plays the role of the genotype?**
A: The loss (how wrong the model's next-word predictions are on real text) is the fitness function, inverted: lower is better. The genotype is the set of weights. Training changes one set of weights step by step; it doesn't select among a population.

**Q2. Why does gradient descent need far fewer evaluations than the evolution strategy in the widget, and why does that matter at the scale of billions of weights?**
A: Each gradient step uses the exact local direction of improvement, while the ES has to discover a direction by sampling λ mutants and keeping the best. With billions of weights, random mutations almost never point downhill, but backpropagation gives the full gradient cheaply.

**Q3. You raised ruggedness and gradient descent started stalling. Name one change that helps the evolutionary strategy and one risk of simply raising gradient descent's step size.**
A: A larger mutation size σ (or more offspring λ) lets the ES jump over small ridges into better basins. A bigger learning rate can carry gradient descent over ridges too, but it can also make it overshoot, bounce around, or skip past the global basin.

**Q4. A post quotes a precise “generalization gap” for a frontier model with no source. How should you use it?**
A: Keep the concept (flat basins tend to generalise better than sharp minima) and treat the specific numbers as unverified until you find a primary source. Promotional threads are pointers, not evidence.
