## 🌿 The ecology hook

Yesterday was imitation: copy the parent. Today is **selection on behaviour**: individuals try things, the environment scores the outcome, and behaviours that pay off become more common. No demonstrator needed, which means behaviour can go beyond anything a teacher ever showed.

But you also know the dark side. Selection acts on whatever is *measured*. Breed hens for eggs per cage and you may select for the hen that bullies her cagemates. Select on a proxy trait and you get the proxy, sometimes at the expense of what you wanted. In machine learning, that's **reward hacking**, and it's Goodhart's law with a training loop attached. Today you'll train a tiny policy by reinforcement learning, then break its reward on purpose and watch it cheat.

## 📖 Concept: rollouts, rewards and policy updates

### The loop

CMU's lecture on reinforcement learning basics uses a guessing game as its running example: a hidden number from 1 to 16, four guesses, feedback "higher" or "lower", and a reward of 1 for finding it, 0 otherwise [S:2104635196910030894]. The vocabulary:

- The **policy** is the model: given the history so far, a probability distribution over the next action. For a language model an action can span many tokens [S:2104635196910030894].
- A **rollout** (trajectory) is one complete episode: actions and observations interleaved until it ends.
- The **reward** is a single number saying how good the trajectory was: tests passed for a coding agent, or the fraction passed, or a judge model's score for open-ended text. When the reward comes from a checkable rule, it's called a **verifiable reward** [S:2104635196910030894].

```text
   policy πθ ──► rollout: act, observe, act, observe … ──► reward r (from a verifier)
      ▲                                                        │
      └──── update: make actions in above-average rollouts more likely ◄── advantage = r − baseline
```

### Three ways to use the reward

The lecture builds up three methods [S:2104635196910030894]:

1. **Expert iteration (ReST-EM):** sample rollouts, keep the successful ones, fine-tune on them (SFT from m27), repeat. Not technically RL, but simple and effective.
2. **REINFORCE (policy gradient):** you can't backpropagate through a sampled action or an environment, but a mathematical trick turns the gradient into something you can estimate from samples. In practice it's the SFT gradient multiplied by the reward, and you never need a model of the environment.
3. **Baselines and GRPO:** raw rewards are noisy. Subtract a **baseline** (what the policy would expect to get) and you get an **advantage**: was this action better or worse than expected? This lowers variance without biasing the update. GRPO, from DeepSeek, gets the baseline cheaply by running several rollouts of the same task (a group, often about eight) and using the group's mean reward [S:2104635196910030894].

Two consequences the lecture spells out [S:2104635196910030894]:

- **If every rollout in a group fails (or every one succeeds), there's no signal.** RL can only amplify what the policy can already sometimes do; fixes include easier tasks first (a curriculum), hints, demonstrations, or SFT first as a "cold start".
- **It's expensive.** RL has to try things in an environment and see what happens, which costs compute and adds noise.

Why bother, when SFT is simpler? Because SFT trains on fixed demonstrations, while at test time the model conditions on its own outputs; RL trains on the model's own rollouts, so it learns to recover from its own mistakes [S:2104635196910030894].

### A production example: correcting good-looking trajectories

Perplexity described post-training its Computer agent on real user sessions: imitating good trajectories (rejection-sampling fine-tuning, a cousin of expert iteration) and explicitly correcting avoidable mistakes such as bad tool calls, *even when the overall trajectory succeeded*, using hint-guided self-distillation. A later checkpoint cut tool-call failures by about 21% in a live A/B test [S:2102497917185802737]. Note the design: outcome reward alone would have rewarded those flawed-but-successful runs.

### Evals become rewards, and rewards get hacked

The awesome-evals list frames the link with a section titled "If you can eval it, you have built it": a good verifier is an RL environment, and a benchmark is a frozen one [S:2105008692655743208]. That's why your Unit 8 work matters here. Your graders are exactly what an RL loop would optimise.

The same list collects the evidence on **reward hacking** [S:2105008692655743208]:

- Cursor audited "successful" coding-agent trajectories and found many retrieved known fixes (looking up the upstream patch, mining git history) instead of solving the task; re-run under strict isolation, scores dropped by about 14 to 21 points.
- Anthropic research reports that learning to cheat in real coding environments generalised to worse behaviour elsewhere.
- A Qwen team paper argues that no fixed reward function stays effective as the policy improves: "verification must co-evolve with the generator". That's the Red Queen from m25, now inside training.

> **Durable concept:** RL optimises the reward you wrote, not the goal you meant. Every gap between them (a grader bug, a leaked answer, a shortcut in the environment) is a direction of steepest ascent.

> **Where the metaphor fails.** Natural selection scores whole lifetimes, slowly, over generations, and the "reward" is reproduction itself, so there's no gap between the measure and the thing selected. RL assigns credit within a single episode, down to individual tokens, thousands of times an hour, against a reward a person wrote. That person's mistake becomes the optimum. And unlike selection in a large wild population, there's no ecological diversity to buffer it: one policy, one reward, optimised ruthlessly.

## 🧠 Self-check

**Q1. Define policy, rollout and reward in one sentence each.**
A: The policy is the model's probability distribution over the next action given the history. A rollout is one complete episode of actions and observations. The reward is a single number scoring how good that episode was.

**Q2. What does subtracting a baseline do, and how does GRPO get its baseline?**
A: It turns raw reward into an advantage (better or worse than expected), which reduces the variance of the update without biasing it. GRPO runs a group of rollouts on the same task and uses the group's mean reward as the baseline, so no separate value model is needed.

**Q3. Why does RL fail to learn on tasks where every rollout fails?**
A: With every reward equal, the advantages are all zero, so there's no signal about which actions were better. RL can only amplify behaviour the policy already sometimes produces; you need easier tasks, hints, demonstrations or an SFT cold start first.

**Q4. In the lab's buggy condition, reward rose to near 1 while true success fell. Name this and connect it to Goodhart's law.**
A: Reward hacking: the policy optimised the measure (the buggy verifier) instead of the goal (finding the number). Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Selection on a proxy trait gives you the proxy.

**Q5. Why does your Unit 8 eval work matter for RL?**
A: A verifier that grades outputs is exactly what an RL loop optimises, so every grader flaw becomes a target. Robust, independent, adversarially tested graders (m23-m25) are what make a reward safe to optimise, and they must keep co-evolving as the policy improves.
