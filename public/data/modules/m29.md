## 🌿 The ecology hook

A **reciprocal transplant** asks a blunt question: does performance in the home site predict performance away? You move each population into the other's environment and measure fitness. Local adaptation shows up as a home advantage that disappears on transplant.

Robot learning lives on that question. Policies and controllers are developed in **simulation** (cheap, safe, endlessly repeatable) and then transplanted to a **real robot**, where friction, masses, cameras and cables are all a little different. The drop in performance is the **sim-to-real gap**. Today you'll see how coding agents are being used on both sides of the transplant, and you'll run a small transplant experiment of your own in a physics simulator.

## 📖 Concept: coding agents in the physical loop

### From one sentence to a trained gait

At the hype end, a post shows one sentence in Claude Code ("train a stable tripod gait for Jumper, replay and evaluate it, then package it") leading the agent to read the repo, train a gait for a 22-joint open-source crab robot in the MuJoCo simulator, replay and check it, then export it [S:2108128859006570832]. Treat it as a pointer: it shows the shape of the workflow (an agent that trains, *evaluates* and packages), not evidence of how reliable that is.

### SimEX: simulation as the agent's laboratory

The SimEX paper (Amazon FAR, UT Austin, CMU) is the primary source to read. It names two failure modes of putting coding agents on robots. Writing control code in one pass ("Code as Policies") fails because the model must guess quantities it can't know, like calibration errors or reachable grasps. Iterating directly on the physical robot works better but is slow, costly and risky: every trial uses robot time, and untested code can cause collisions [S:2107485908673106241].

Their answer is to use simulation as a bridge, in two stages [S:2107485908673106241]:

1. **Open-ended autoresearch in simulation.** The agent probes its current "toolbox" (a `toolbox.py` of perception and control primitives plus a `skill.md` of usage notes and measured limits) on self-generated task and environment variations, each with an executable success test; picks one systematic weakness; and hill-climbs the toolbox on it. A **capability bank** of previously solved tasks is replayed during each round so that new edits don't erase old skills, and several independent optimisation runs explore in parallel, sharing short descriptions of their experiments but not copying each other's code.
2. **Physical adaptation with a tiny budget.** A few real trials; each real failure is used to correct the simulator and to screen candidate repairs in simulation before the next real attempt.

The headline: with about 10 minutes of real-robot interaction per task and no demonstrations, SimEX succeeded in 26 of 30 physical evaluation trials across towel folding, barcode scanning and plate handling, where the strongest baseline succeeded in 3 [S:2107485908673106241]. The authors' interpretation is the durable idea: the simulator needs only to get the *qualitative* physics right, because what transfers is knowledge and procedures (measured limits, strategies that work), not a single policy tuned to one simulated scene [S:2107485908673106241]. Their stated limitations: one robot platform, and a human still resets the scene between physical trials [S:2107485908673106241].

### RPG: practice, diagnose, rewrite your own skills

Berkeley and Amazon's RPG ("Guided Self-Improvement for Embodied Agents") runs a related loop: reconstruct practice tasks from a real demonstration, practise in simulation guided by the demo video, and update a shared skill library from failures, then go back to the real robot. The authors report 30 of 30 real-robot successes across 3 tasks, and 95% success across 22 simulated manipulation tasks after 15 rounds of self-improvement [S:2107871108338982988]. This is m18's self-rewriting skill library, now with a body.

### Which data actually teaches a robot?

Varun Nair's "Robotics Data Map" argues that the usual data pyramid (lots of cheap internet video at the bottom, a little expensive robot deployment data at the top) squashes several properties onto one axis [S:2090126022465495083]. He separates them:

- **Action-space distance:** how far the data is from what the robot will execute. First-person human video has no robot actions at all; handheld-gripper capture is closer; teleoperation of the actual robot is zero distance.
- **The fence:** who is acting. Human demonstrations, however close, show the *human's* choices. Only **rollouts** (the policy acting) contain the policy's own mistakes, and "you find out a policy is broken by running it and watching it fail" [S:2090126022465495083].
- **Substrate:** atoms or bits. Any data type can be simulated. His bet is that simulated rollouts supply the volume of on-policy failure data that real rollouts can't, while noting the sim-to-real gap is still an active blocker [S:2090126022465495083].

```text
              human acting  │  policy acting (rollouts)
 real    ego video → UMI → teleop │ real rollouts  (scarce, costly, informative)
 ─────────────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────────────────
 sim       (rarely worth it)      │ sim rollouts   (volume; trust only if sim ≈ real)
                         ← action-space distance shrinks toward the fence
```

### Report robot results like an ecologist

Robot success rates are binomial data with small *n* and big consequences. SimEX reports successes out of 25 scheduled trials per task (5 rollouts × 5 seeds) with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals [S:2107485908673106241]. The Reality Check leaderboard launched with 14,400 real-world rollouts across four models, with confidence intervals [S:2104832660791308542]. Same lesson as m23 and m25: a number without its interval is an anecdote.

> **Where the metaphor fails.** In a reciprocal transplant, organisms keep adapting, and the "home" environment is a real place. A sim-trained controller doesn't adapt when transplanted: it has a fixed gap to a reality it never saw, and the only ways to close it are a better simulator, real data, or an explicit adaptation step like SimEX's Stage 2. And the simulator is a model *you* wrote, so a controller can exploit its inaccuracies, the way a policy exploits a buggy reward (m28). SimEX's authors say this directly: capabilities optimised purely in simulation can exploit the simulator's inaccuracies [S:2107485908673106241].

## 🛠 Lab: a two-link arm and a transplant experiment (15 min)

Everything runs on your CPU with free software (`pip install mujoco` in a virtual environment).

**Part A (3 min, phone OK).** Design the transplant: list three physical parameters you'd perturb to make an "away" simulator (for example link mass, joint damping, actuator strength) and predict which one hurts a tuned controller most. Write the prediction down.

**Part B (12 min, at a computer).** Paste into Claude Code or Codex:

```text
Create ~/learn-ai/labs/m29 and work only inside it, in a Python venv (pip install mujoco numpy only).
1. reach.py: a planar 2-link arm defined in inline MJCF (links 0.3 m and 0.25 m, torque-controlled hinges,
   a fingertip site). Task: from a fixed start pose, bring the fingertip within 2 cm of a random reachable
   target and hold it there for 0.2 s, within 2 s of sim time. Success predicate computed from sim state.
2. controller.py: joint-space PD control toward the analytic inverse-kinematics solution; gains kp, kd.
3. tune.py ("home" sim): grid-search kp, kd on 30 training targets (seed 1), choose the best by success
   rate then mean time-to-target. Save gains.json and a short skill.md noting the measured limits
   (for example targets near full extension).
4. evaluate.py: 100 held-out targets (seed 2). Report success with a 95% Wilson interval and mean time.
5. Transplant: build an "away" sim with [my three perturbations] (one at a time, then all together).
   Evaluate the frozen home gains in each. Then allow an adaptation budget of 5 trials in the away sim
   to re-tune kp/kd, and re-evaluate on the 100 held-out targets.
6. results.md: a table home / away (each perturbation) / away-adapted with intervals, and two sentences
   on which perturbation caused the biggest gap and whether 5 trials closed it.
Optional: render a short video or GIF of 3 reaches if it works without extra installs.
```

**What to check in the result**

- Home performance: is the held-out success rate close to the training one? If it's much lower, the gains overfit the 30 training targets (m23 again).
- Transplant: which perturbation produced the largest drop? Did your Part A prediction hold?
- Adaptation: did 5 away-trials recover most of the gap? Do the intervals overlap with the home result?
- Read `skill.md`. Is it something a future agent could use, SimEX-style, to avoid known failure regions?

## 🧠 Self-check

**Q1. What are the two problems SimEX identifies with putting coding agents directly on robots?**
A: Writing control code in one pass fails because the model must guess physical quantities it can't know (calibration, reachable grasps). Iterating on the physical robot is slow, costly and potentially unsafe, since every trial uses robot time and untested code can collide.

**Q2. Why can SimEX work with a simulator that is only "roughly correct"?**
A: Because it transfers knowledge and procedures (a toolbox of primitives plus measured limits and strategies), not one policy fitted to one simulated scene; and it corrects the simulator with a few real trials in Stage 2.

**Q3. In the Robotics Data Map, what's on the other side of "the fence", and why does it matter?**
A: Rollouts: data where the policy, not a human, is acting. Only rollouts contain the policy's own mistakes, which is the signal that teaches recovery; human demonstrations, however close to the robot's action space, can't supply it.

**Q4. A paper reports 9/10 real-robot successes. What should you ask?**
A: For the interval and the protocol: how many trials, seeds and tasks, whether evaluation tasks were held out, and the confidence interval. 9/10 has a wide 95% interval (roughly 60% to 98%).

**Q5. How does the reciprocal-transplant analogy break for sim-to-real?**
A: Transplanted organisms keep adapting and the home site is real. A sim-trained controller has a fixed gap to a reality it never saw, and can exploit simulator inaccuracies, so the gap only closes through better simulation, real data or an explicit adaptation step.
