## 🌿 The ecology hook

"It takes all the running you can do, to keep in the same place." Van Valen borrowed the Red Queen's line for a reason you know well: hosts and parasites, predators and prey keep each other honest. A prey population with no predators loses its vigilance; a defence that is never tested decays. Adaptation is maintained by an opponent.

AI systems need opponents too. A generator that only ever checks its own work drifts towards whatever it already believes. Today you'll add two opponents to your eval suite: an **independent verifier** that checks each claim against evidence, and an **adversary** whose only job is to break things. Then you'll measure whether they actually catch more than self-checking does, and report the result with a confidence interval.

## 📖 Concept: never let the generator grade its own homework

### Why self-checking fails

Ryven's article on graph engineering describes the moment this became real. They had built a fan-out where every worker checked its own output before moving on, and everything looked clean. Then they read the research: models can recognise their own writing at well above chance, and that recognition shifts them toward preferring it (the article cites Panickssery et al., NeurIPS 2024, and a judge study by Zheng et al. that measured models favouring their own answers) [S:2104873702198538738]. The same hand was holding the pen for both the essay and the score [S:2104873702198538738].

The fix was a separate node with one job: try to kill each finding before it moves downstream [S:2104873702198538738]. Promix's 14-step graph-engineering guide gives the same rule as "make findings survive an attack": instead of asking one agent whether a finding is correct, create independent critics whose job is to destroy it (one checks correctness, one tries to reproduce it, one searches for contradictory evidence) and only pass the survivors on [S:2106462125874987099].

### Three details that decide whether a verifier works

**1. Fresh context.** Ryven's first verifier received the same conversation the worker had used, and agreed with everything, because it had already seen the reasoning and was primed to accept it. A verifier that shares context with the worker isn't checking anything [S:2104873702198538738]. Give it only the claim and the evidence, in a new session.

**2. Several lenses, then a vote.** The version that "caught anything real" ran three fresh verifiers with different lenses (correctness, recency, source validity), each returning keep or drop, with a majority required [S:2104873702198538738].

**3. An anchor outside the models.** Even with paired verifiers, Ryven found an audit checking its numbers against a report that came from the same underlying source: "everything agreed with everything else" and nothing was verified [S:2104873702198538738]. What breaks the echo is an anchor that can't be argued with: a test that actually ran and passed, a number in a real record, a few rules frozen so an optimiser can't quietly weaken them [S:2104873702198538738]. For you, the anchor is often the source text itself, or your own labelled data.

### Test the tester: break it on purpose

Before trusting their judge script, Ryven took a known-good output, deleted a required section, and checked that the judge printed FAIL. If the deliberately broken file had passed, the judge was blind, and every green result afterwards meant nothing [S:2104873702198538738]. This is the **planted-error** method you'll use in the lab: inject errors whose location you know, and measure what fraction each checker catches.

```text
           writer ──► report (claims + citations)
                         │
          plant k known errors (key kept hidden)
                         │
     ┌───────────────────┼─────────────────────────┐
     ▼                   ▼                         ▼
 SELF-CHECK          VERIFIER                  ADVERSARY ×3 lenses
 (writer's own       (fresh session:           (fresh sessions: "try to
  context)            claim + cited source)     disprove", majority vote)
     │                   │                         │
     └─────── caught / k, false alarms / true claims, with 95% CI ───────┘
```

### Adversaries as teammates

Claude Code's agent-teams documentation recommends the same structure for debugging: when the root cause is unclear, a single agent tends to find one plausible explanation and stop looking, so you spawn teammates that each investigate a hypothesis *and* challenge the others' [S:2105764769479455043]. The docs are also honest about the cost: each teammate has its own context window, so token use scales with the number of teammates, and teams pay off for research and review rather than routine work [S:2105764769479455043].

A popular post in the pack suggests a dedicated adversary that only steps in at three moments: before an interface contract is locked, when a test breaks twice ("are we patching the bug or just hiding the symptom?"), and before anyone calls the work done [S:2105764769479455043]. The three checkpoints are a useful pattern. Treat the post's specific setup (model assignments, command-line flags, latency numbers) as unverified: it's a pointer, not documentation.

### Report results with intervals

Robotics is going through the same shift. The Reality Check benchmark launched as a public leaderboard of real-world robot manipulation: 14,400 real rollouts across four models, multiple tasks and data regimes, reported **with confidence intervals** [S:2104832660791308542]. Its framing, "the Era of Evals is coming to AI robotics" [S:2104832660791308542], applies to your work too: a catch rate of 5 out of 6 is a very different claim from 50 out of 60.

> **Background (not from the pack; standard statistics):** for a catch rate of *x* out of *n*, use a Wilson 95% interval. 5/6 gives roughly 44% to 97%; 50/60 gives roughly 72% to 91%. If two checkers' intervals overlap heavily, you haven't shown that one is better. Plant more errors or repeat the run.

> **Where the metaphor fails.** In nature nobody referees the arms race; selection on both sides is real and relentless. In an eval you build *both* sides, so you control how hard the adversary pushes, and it's very easy to build a weak one that gives false comfort: an adversary that shares your generator's model, context or sources will tend to miss exactly the errors the generator makes. Natural enemies are also independent lineages; your verifier and generator may be the same model in different sessions. That's why the planted-error test matters: it measures the adversary's strength instead of assuming it.

```text
Create ~/learn-ai/labs/m25 and work only inside it. Python standard library only.
1. Pick 3 chunks from learn-ai-rag/corpus/chunks.jsonl about evals (use corpus/query.py "eval grader held-out").
   Using `claude -p`, write report.md: 12 short factual claims, each ending with the chunk id it relies on.
2. plant.py (deterministic, seed 25): copy the report to planted.md and introduce exactly 6 errors in
   6 different claims, one of each type: changed number, reversed claim, wrong citation (points to the
   wrong chunk), unsupported addition, overgeneralisation ("always"/"never"), and my type: [paste yours].
   Save the answer key to key.json and never show key.json to any checker.
3. Three checkers, each outputs a JSON list of claim numbers it flags, with a one-line reason each:
   a) SELF-CHECK: continue the writer's session if the CLI allows it (`claude -c -p`); otherwise give
      one call the original writing prompt plus planted.md and say "you wrote this; check your work".
   b) VERIFIER: one fresh `claude -p` call per claim, given ONLY the claim and the full text of the
      chunk it cites. Return keep/drop and why.
   c) ADVERSARY: per claim, three fresh calls with different lenses (correctness, citation validity,
      overclaiming), each told to try to disprove the claim; drop if 2 of 3 say drop.
      Use `codex exec` for these if installed, to vary the model family.
4. score.py: for each checker, planted errors caught (/6) and false alarms on the 6 clean claims,
   each with a 95% Wilson interval. Write results.md with the table and every miss explained.
5. Sanity test: verifier.py must flag a claim I know is wrong (make one with a wrong number from
   chunk text) and keep one I know is right. Fail loudly if not.
Package the verifier and adversary as functions in checkers.py so my capstone can import them.
```

**What to check in the result**

- Did the sanity test pass? If the verifier keeps a claim you know is wrong, it's blind, and its other results mean nothing [S:2104873702198538738].
- Compare catches: self-check versus verifier versus adversary. Which error types did self-checking miss?
- Look at false alarms too. An adversary that flags everything catches all planted errors and is useless. Count both columns.
- Look at the intervals. With 6 planted errors, can you actually claim one checker is better? Write one sentence about how many errors you'd need to plant to tell them apart.

## 🧠 Self-check

**Q1. Why does a verifier need a fresh context rather than the worker's conversation?**
A: A verifier that has seen the worker's reasoning is primed to accept it and tends to agree with everything. Given only the claim and the evidence in a new session, it judges the claim on its merits.

**Q2. Your three verifiers all agree a figure is correct, but all three checked it against the same summary the worker produced. What's wrong?**
A: They share an upstream source, so their agreement is an echo, not independent confirmation. You need an anchor outside the model chain: the original data, the primary source, or a test that actually runs.

**Q3. How do you check that a judge or verifier isn't blind?**
A: Break something on purpose. Plant known errors (or feed a known-bad output) and confirm the checker flags them, while keeping known-good items. Report catch rate and false-alarm rate.

**Q4. A verifier catches 5 of 6 planted errors and self-checking catches 3 of 6. Can you conclude the verifier is better?**
A: Not confidently. With n = 6 the 95% intervals are very wide and overlap heavily. Plant more errors or repeat across several reports before claiming a difference.

**Q5. How does the Red Queen analogy break for adversarial evals?**
A: Natural arms races have independent opponents and no referee. In evals you build both sides, so a weak adversary (same model, same context, same sources as the generator) can give false comfort. You have to measure the adversary's strength, for example with planted errors.
