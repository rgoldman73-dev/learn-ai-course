## 🌿 The ecology hook

Yesterday's forager could only find a patch by its exact landmark. Many animals do better. A shearwater crossing open ocean can't see the krill, but it can smell the plume of dimethyl sulphide that rises where phytoplankton is being grazed, and fly up the gradient. It doesn't need an exact match; it needs a **similarity signal** that gets stronger as it gets closer to what it wants.

**Embeddings** give search the same sense. Each chunk of text becomes a point in a high-dimensional space, placed so that passages with similar meaning sit near each other. A question becomes a point too, and search means “find the nearest chunks”, even when they share no words with the question. Today you'll add that sense of smell to your pack search, combine it with yesterday's keyword sight, and test whether the combination actually finds more of what you need.

## 📖 Concept: meaning as distance

### What an embedding is

Ronin's roadmap defines a text embedding as a piece of text projected into a high-dimensional vector space, where similar text produces similar vectors, usually compared with **cosine similarity** (the angle between two vectors) [S:2033587293064204349]. Its suggested first exercise is “RAG in miniature”: embed 20 related sentences and return the three nearest to a query [S:2033587293064204349].

The models that make these vectors can be small enough to run on a laptop. EmbeddingGemma 2, announced in the pack, is an open embedding model designed to run on-device in about half a gigabyte of RAM, and it's natively multimodal: code, images, audio and video share one embedding space with text [S:2107516198959386895]. (That's the announcement's description; check its size and licence yourself before relying on it.)

One practical rule from the LightRAG documentation: **choose the embedding model before you index**, and use the same one for queries. Vectors from one model are meaningless to another, so changing models means re-embedding every chunk [S:2106462125874987099].

### Why not just use embeddings?

Because each sense has blind spots. BM25 can't see synonyms; embeddings can blur exact names, numbers and IDs, and they rank by *topical* similarity, which isn't the same as *answering the question*. The production RAG course's comparison table puts it like this: BM25 for exact keyword matching, vector search for semantic similarity, and hybrid for the best overall relevance [S:2105259648001376474]. Its week 4 builds exactly that hybrid: section-based chunks, 1024-dimensional embeddings, and **reciprocal rank fusion (RRF)** to combine the two rankings, with an automatic fall-back to BM25 if the embedding service is unavailable [S:2105259648001376474].

Graphiti, one of the agent-memory projects in the pack, does the same with three senses at once: semantic embeddings, BM25 keywords and graph traversal [S:2105607355262009622].

### Reciprocal rank fusion

```text
 query ──▶ BM25 ranking:    A  B  C  D  E ...
       └─▶ vector ranking:  C  F  A  G  B ...
                  │
     RRF score(doc) = Σ over rankings of 1 / (k + rank)      (background: k is often 60)
                  │
                  ▼
 fused:  A (1/61 + 1/63)   C (1/63 + 1/61)   B (1/62 + 1/65)   F (1/62)  ...
```

> **Background (standard method, not from the pack):** RRF ignores the raw scores, which live on different scales (BM25 scores versus cosine similarities), and uses only each document's **rank** in each list. A document that's near the top of both lists wins; one that's top in one list and absent from the other still gets credit. The constant `k` (60 is a common default) stops the very top rank from dominating.

The production course notes that its fusion weighting is configurable, so you can lean towards keywords or meaning [S:2105259648001376474].

### Two-stage retrieval and agentic retrieval

Fusion isn't the only way to combine signals. Ronin describes the **retrieve-then-rerank** pattern: a fast first stage (keyword or embedding search) returns candidates, then a slower, more accurate **cross-encoder reranker** re-scores the top few by reading query and passage together [S:2033587293064204349]. OpenScholar, the scientific-literature system in the pack, runs a retriever followed by a reranker, and its authors mention a combined sparse-dense (keyword-plus-embedding) retriever for their demo [S:2106462125874987099].

And an agent can make retrieval a loop rather than a single call. The production course's week 7 “agentic RAG” lets the model decide whether retrieval is needed at all, **grades** the retrieved documents for relevance, and **rewrites the query and tries again** if they're not relevant [S:2105259648001376474]. That's a forager that can change patches when the smell was misleading.

### Measure before you believe

Every one of these additions costs time, money or complexity, and the only way to know whether it helped is to test on your own questions. The pack's verification list includes tools built for this, such as Ragas and DeepEval for evaluating RAG pipelines, and PaperQA2 for answers grounded in papers with citations [S:2106462125874987099]. Today you'll do it by hand with yesterday's labelled questions: an A/B test, same questions, three retrievers.

> **Where the metaphor fails.** A smell gradient is anchored in chemistry: DMS really is more concentrated near grazing plankton. Embedding similarity is learned from text statistics, so “near” means “written about in similar ways”, not “true” or “useful”. A fluent passage that mentions all the right concepts, but answers a different question, can sit closer to your query than the dry paragraph with the answer. And unlike an odour plume, the space can't be checked against reality: you only find out by labelling hits. That's why hybrid search keeps the keyword “eyes”, and why you measure.

## 🧠 Self-check

**Q1. What is a text embedding, and how is similarity usually measured?**
A: A vector representation of text in a high-dimensional space where texts with similar meaning lie close together; similarity is usually cosine similarity, the angle between two vectors.

**Q2. Give one thing BM25 does better than embeddings and one thing embeddings do better.**
A: BM25: exact terms, names, IDs and numbers, with interpretable matches. Embeddings: paraphrases and synonyms, finding relevant passages that share no words with the query.

**Q3. Why does reciprocal rank fusion use ranks instead of raw scores?**
A: BM25 scores and cosine similarities are on different, incomparable scales. Ranks put both lists on the same footing, so documents high in both lists rise, and a document strong in only one list still gets credit.

**Q4. You switch to a better embedding model next month. What must you do, and why?**
A: Re-embed every chunk with the new model (and embed queries with the same model). Vectors from different models live in different spaces and can't be compared.

**Q5. Why is “embeddings are a sense of smell” misleading?**
A: Smell follows a real chemical gradient tied to the resource. Embedding similarity is learned from text statistics, so a fluent passage on the right topic that doesn't answer the question can rank highly. You only find out by checking hits against labelled questions.
